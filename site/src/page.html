Abstract Safety alignment for Large Language Models is heavily focused on English with limited multilingual representation, particularly damaging low-resource language performance. Related open training datasets are often limited in size or do not follow region-localized harmful topics to broaden the scope of alignment beyond cultures. We propose a scalable template-based augmentation pipeline to produce massive multilingual refusal and benign corpora, extracting safe templates from harmful prompts, translating them and then filling in the slots with culturally authentic entities, both harmful and benign. As a result, we release MSafeTemp, a validated corpus spanning 60 languages useful for multilingual safety alignment and evaluation. Using the dataset, we show that English-only evaluation misjudges multilingual safety: for several models, attacks that fail in English succeed once translated, while culturally localized variants expose further failures that translation alone misses. We also confirm that augmenting safety training data through templates allows for higher diversity and quality of alignment, resulting in improved safety performance beyond English. MSafeTemp and the respective pipeline are aimed at facilitating research and development of safer and more responsible models for less-represented cultures.

Content note. This page shows harmful prompts from the dataset, including slurs and extremist group names in a few localized examples. They come from the public HarmBench and WildGuardMix sources or are derived from them, and none carries operational detail.

Method

Each seed becomes up to 15 harmful variants per language, plus benign and localized ones. A strongly aligned model will usually refuse to translate or adapt a harmful prompt out-of-the-box. Initially, we ask a language model to cut out the few words that make the prompt harmful and leave a safe template with typed placeholder slots. The template is translated with its slots kept fixed, paraphrased several times, and only then filled again inside the target language. Every writing stage is checked by a judge from a different model family to ensure correctness, authenticity and adherence to original intent class.

    † Judge step: gpt-oss-120b checks the output of the step before it. Paraphrases and fills are also judged inside their own steps.

    Findings

    Three results from red-teaming seven open models. Each model answers every prompt of both evaluation tracks with greedy decoding; gpt-oss-safeguard-120b judges each response. Rates are means over languages.

    1

    English-only evaluation misjudges multilingual safety

    Most of the open models show on average several times larger ASR beyond English. Olmo-3.1-32B-Instruct never complies with an original HarmBench behavior in English, yet its attack success rises from 0.0% to 13.7% once the same behaviors are translated.

    Attack success rate, % (lower is safer).

    2

    Localized prompts reach failures that translation misses

    PH and TR prompts share seed, template, language and judge; PH only swaps a fill for a local referent and may rephrase around it. With the wording held fixed, the two break models about equally often but agree on fewer than half of the behaviors they break and about 45% of arm-specific failures happen only with local entities. We find culturally-adapted breaks more beneficial to broaden coverage, but not as a stronger attack: it lowers attack success for most frontier models. The German worked example shows one failure reachable only with a local entity.

    HarmBench attack success, %, per language.

    3

    Low attack success often comes with over-refusal

    A model can reach low attack success by refusing harmless requests too. gpt-oss-20b refuses 32 to 45% of benign pairs while staying near zero attack success. The pattern follows: fully open-source models struggle to balance harmless and helpful behavior much more compared to open-weight models.

    Each point is one model. Horizontal: attack success on harmful prompts. Vertical: over-refusal on their benign DIRECT twins. Both %, both lower is better.

    Training

    After SFT and DPO, multilingual HarmBench attack success falls to between 1.3% and 2.9% in every family. Each checkpoint is fine-tuned for one epoch on refusals and benign answers from the WildGuard train split, mixed with general instruction data, then trained with DPO on preference pairs sampled from itself. SFT cuts attack success on both MSafeTemp tracks in every family except Apertus-v1.5 on HarmBench, which starts low and moves from 2.3% to 3.0% before DPO brings it to 1.3%. DPO on the model's own samples then recovers much of the over-refusal SFT adds, fully so for the Apertus instruct checkpoints.
